# Genetic Algorithm Strategy Engine for UAV Disaster Survey

## Abstract
This project implements a Genetic Algorithm (GA) to plan the waypoint routes, relative speed settings, and sensor power profiles of a small unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) fleet operating in a synthetic disaster environment. The environment is represented by a 50 × 50 grid with blocked cells, high-priority survey regions, target locations, and graded hazard pockets. A hybrid chromosome encodes continuous coordinates and control settings. Tournament selection, segment crossover, spatial mutation, elitism, and repair are used to evolve plans. The objective rewards priority-weighted coverage and target detection while penalizing energy use, mission duration, hazard exposure, and constraint violations. A random-search baseline and hyperparameter sensitivity experiments provide comparative evaluation. This is a simulation prototype rather than a deployable flight-control system.

## 1. Problem formulation and environment
The mission planning problem is difficult because the objectives conflict. Visiting more regions can improve coverage and detection but normally increases travel distance, energy consumption, and exposure to hazards. Faster flight can reduce elapsed time but may increase the modeled energy cost. Sensor activation can improve coverage while consuming additional energy. A weighted objective is used to compare candidate plans with one scalar score.

The operational area is discretized into a 50 × 50 matrix. Each cell may be open, blocked, hazardous, or part of a high-priority region. Six synthetic target coordinates are used to calculate detection performance. Three UAVs begin at fixed corner-adjacent positions. The environment is generated deterministically with a fixed random seed, so comparisons use the same map. Hazard intensity is continuous between zero and one, while obstacle cells are binary.

## 2. Chromosome representation
Each individual is a three-dimensional array of shape `(number_of_drones, number_of_waypoints, 4)`. Each waypoint gene is `(x, y, v, s)`: grid coordinate `x`, grid coordinate `y`, relative speed `v`, and sensor power `s`. In the demo, there are three drones and eight waypoints per drone. Coordinates are bounded by the grid; speed is restricted to `[0.5, 2.0]`; sensor power is restricted to `[0, 1]`. The representation is hybrid: coordinates and speed are real-valued, and sensor power is continuous but can be interpreted as an activation level. A value near zero represents a mostly inactive sensor and a value near one represents full power.

The representation was chosen because it is compact, supports smooth spatial mutations, and allows the genetic operators to modify route segments without rebuilding the entire plan. Each drone has its own segment, so a route from one parent can be inherited while other drone routes remain unchanged. The limitation is that the genes describe waypoints, not a complete dynamically feasible aircraft trajectory; vehicle turning radius, acceleration, and communication constraints are not modeled.

## 3. Fitness function and constraint handling
The scalar objective is:

`F = 0.35 C + 0.25 D − 0.15 E_norm − 0.10 T_norm − 0.15 H_norm − P`.

`C` is the fraction of priority-weighted grid cells covered by sensor footprints. `D` is the average modeled detection probability over the target list. `E_norm` is normalized energy expenditure, modeled as a function of segment distance, relative speed, and sensor power. `T_norm` is normalized travel time, calculated from segment distance divided by relative speed. `H_norm` is integrated hazard intensity sampled along route segments. `P` is a penalty for segments crossing blocked cells and for route-length violations. The coverage and detection terms receive a combined weight of 0.60 because the simulated mission emphasizes locating disaster areas and targets. Energy, time, and hazard weights sum to 0.40, so efficiency and risk remain material.

These weights are initial engineering choices, not universal constants. A real application would require stakeholders to set mission priorities, normalize objectives using defensible operating ranges, and potentially use a Pareto-based algorithm such as NSGA-II rather than a single weighted sum. Weighted sums can hide solutions that are strong on one objective but weak on another.

Constraint handling combines repair and penalties. Waypoints are clipped to map boundaries, moved to the nearest free cell if placed in an obstacle, and speed/sensor genes are clamped to legal ranges. When a route exceeds the approximate maximum range, later waypoint coordinates are pulled toward the start. Segment intersections with blocked cells are sampled and penalized. This hybrid method makes most chromosomes easier to evaluate while preserving a penalty for infeasible connecting segments. It is not a formal guarantee of obstacle-free paths; a production planner should use a grid-search repair method such as A* between every consecutive waypoint and then re-evaluate range.

## 4. Genetic operators and termination
The initial population is generated randomly with bounded genes. Tournament selection samples a small group and chooses its highest-fitness member. The crossover operator copies a contiguous waypoint block from one parent into the other, and occasionally inherits a complete drone route. This segment-level design preserves local route patterns more often than independently mixing every coordinate. Mutation adds Gaussian perturbations to spatial coordinates and control genes. A small fraction of spatial mutations re-sample a waypoint globally, helping the population escape local regions. Repair and evaluation follow each offspring operation. Elitism carries the top two candidates into the next generation unchanged.

The main run uses a population size of 35, mutation rate of 0.12 per gene field, tournament size of three, and a maximum of 60 generations in the main experiment. The algorithm also stops after 25 consecutive generations without a meaningful improvement. Fixed seeds support repeatable experiments. The settings are a starting point; the sensitivity experiment varies population size, mutation rate, and tournament size.

## 5. Experiments and interpretation
The main program saves a best-versus-average convergence curve, an evolved path map, a sensor coverage heatmap, and a best-score comparison against random search. Random search evaluates independently generated candidates under the same fitness function and map, using a matched approximate evaluation budget. For a fairer comparison, the baseline uses approximately the same number of candidate evaluations as the GA (initial population plus offspring evaluations across completed generations). Because GA reuses information through selection and inheritance, it may improve promising partial route patterns over generations; random search does not exploit parent fitness.

The sensitivity script runs 12 generations per configuration and tests population sizes of 15, 25, and 35; mutation rates of 0.04, 0.12, and 0.25; and tournament sizes of 2, 3, and 5. It writes exact outcomes to `outputs/sensitivity_results.csv`. Larger populations can improve diversity but increase runtime. Very low mutation may cause premature convergence, while very high mutation can destroy useful inherited route segments. Higher tournament size increases selection pressure, often accelerating early improvement but risking loss of diversity. These are hypotheses to assess against the CSV results; the final submission should quote actual measured values rather than assuming every trend occurs.

The current comparison reports the best GA score and the best score from random search. This is useful as a simple baseline but is not a complete statistical comparison. A stronger study would run at least 10 independent seeds per configuration and report mean, standard deviation, and confidence intervals for best fitness, coverage, detection, energy, time, and hazard exposure. The random baseline and GA should also be matched by total fitness evaluations, not merely wall-clock time.

## 6. Results
The recorded main run completed 60 generations. The best GA fitness was **0.1974**, compared with **−4.7165** for the best of 2,015 random-search candidates. In this run, the evolved plan achieved a priority-weighted coverage score of **0.2618** and mean target detection score of **0.8053**. The modeled total energy expenditure was **293.0** units, flight time was **250.1** units, and integrated hazard exposure was **12.69** units. These values are outputs of the chosen synthetic model, not real-world performance guarantees. The GA result is better under the implemented scalar objective, but a single run does not establish statistical superiority.

The sensitivity experiment used 12 generations per configuration and one seed for each configuration. Among population sizes 15, 25, and 35, the best scores were −0.4213, −1.4793, and 0.0815 respectively; the largest tested population performed best in this short run. Mutation rates 0.04, 0.12, and 0.25 yielded 0.1204, −0.0666, and −0.1622 respectively, so the lowest tested mutation rate performed best in this run. Tournament sizes 2, 3, and 5 yielded 0.0196, 0.1021, and 0.1018; tournament sizes 3 and 5 were nearly tied. Since each setting was tested once for only 12 generations, these are preliminary observations rather than robust conclusions. A stronger study should repeat each configuration across multiple seeds and report mean and standard deviation.

The generated figures are `outputs/convergence.png`, `outputs/flight_paths.png`, `outputs/coverage_heatmap.png`, and `outputs/random_vs_ga.png`. Machine-readable details are stored in `outputs/results.json` and `outputs/sensitivity_results.csv`.

## 7. Conclusion and future work
The project demonstrates an end-to-end GA workflow for multi-UAV survey planning: a custom chromosome, objective evaluation, tailored genetic operators, constraint handling, reproducible simulation, baseline comparison, and hyperparameter experiments. Its main strength is modularity: the environment, chromosome, fitness evaluator, GA, and visualization code are separated. Important limitations remain. The map and energy model are synthetic, route-segment obstacle avoidance is penalty-based, and the scalar fitness score does not represent the full Pareto trade-off surface. Future work should use A* repair for all route segments, model inter-UAV separation and vehicle dynamics, evaluate uncertainty in detection, conduct repeated-seed statistics, and consider NSGA-II to expose a set of non-dominated mission plans.
